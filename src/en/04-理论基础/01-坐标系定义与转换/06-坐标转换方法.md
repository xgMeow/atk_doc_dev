# Coordinate Transformation Methods

Coordinate transformation is a fundamental operation in aerospace mission analysis, involving the conversion of vectors or states from one coordinate system to another. This chapter introduces the mathematical principles, common methods, and implementation of coordinate transformations in ATK.

## Basic Concepts of Coordinate Transformation

### Transformation vs. Translation/Rotation

- **Coordinate Transformation**: The re-expression of the same physical quantity in different coordinate systems. In aerospace applications, the vast majority of coordinate transformations are **pure rotations** (the origins of the two coordinate systems are the same), involving only the application of a direction cosine matrix
- **Coordinate Translation/Rotation**: A broader concept that may involve translation of the origin, rotation of the coordinate axes, or a combination of both. When origins differ (e.g., between heliocentric and geocentric systems), both rotation and translation must be considered

::: tip Convention
In this text, "coordinate transformation" refers to the re-expression of the same physical vector in different coordinate systems (including rotation and possibly translation), without changing the magnitude or direction of the physical quantity itself.
:::

### Direction Cosine Matrix (DCM)

The Direction Cosine Matrix is the fundamental tool for describing the rotational relationship between two coordinate systems and is the mathematical core of coordinate transformation.

Let coordinate system A and coordinate system B be given. The elements of the transformation matrix $\mathbf{R}_{BA}$ from A to B are:

$$
R_{BA,ij} = \hat{b}_i \cdot \hat{a}_j = \cos\theta_{ij}
$$

where $\hat{a}_j$ is the $j$-th basis vector of system A, $\hat{b}_i$ is the $i$-th basis vector of system B, and $\theta_{ij}$ is the angle between the two basis vectors.

Properties of the transformation matrix:
- **Orthogonality**: $\mathbf{R}^{-1} = \mathbf{R}^T$, i.e., $\mathbf{R} \mathbf{R}^T = \mathbf{I}$
- **Determinant**: $\det(\mathbf{R}) = +1$ (proper rotation, no reflection)
- **Transitivity**: $\mathbf{R}_{CA} = \mathbf{R}_{CB} \cdot \mathbf{R}_{BA}$ (matrix multiplication direction follows the transformation path from right to left)
- **Group property**: All 3×3 matrices satisfying the above conditions form the SO(3) group

### Vector Transformation

For a vector $\vec{v}$, transforming from coordinate system A to coordinate system B (same origin, rotation only):

$$
\vec{v}_B = \mathbf{R}_{BA} \cdot \vec{v}_A
$$

If the origins of the two coordinate systems differ (e.g., heliocentric ICRF vs. geocentric GCRF), the origin displacement must be added:

$$
\vec{v}_B = \mathbf{R}_{BA} \cdot \vec{v}_A - \mathbf{R}_{BA} \cdot \vec{r}_{O_A \to O_B}^{(A)}
$$

where $\vec{r}_{O_A \to O_B}^{(A)}$ is the position vector of the origin of system B expressed in system A.

### State Transformation

For a state $[\vec{r}, \vec{v}]^\top$ (position and velocity), if the two coordinate systems share the same origin but have a relative rotational angular velocity:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vec{r}_B &= \mathbf{R}_{BA} \cdot \vec{r}_A \\[4pt]
\vec{v}_B &= \mathbf{R}_{BA} \cdot \vec{v}_A + \dot{\mathbf{R}}_{BA} \cdot \vec{r}_A
\end{aligned}
$$

Alternatively, using the angular velocity $\vec{\omega}_{BA}$ (angular velocity of system B relative to system A, expressed in system B):

$$
\vec{v}_B = \mathbf{R}_{BA} \cdot \vec{v}_A + \vec{\omega}_{BA} \times \vec{r}_B
$$

where the second term $\vec{\omega}_{BA} \times \vec{r}_B$ is the **transport velocity**, which is nonzero only when there is relative rotation between the coordinate systems.

If the origins differ and there is relative motion, the origin displacement and velocity terms must be added:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vec{r}_B &= \mathbf{R}_{BA} \cdot \vec{r}_A - \mathbf{R}_{BA} \cdot \vec{r}_{O_A \to O_B}^{(A)} \\
\vec{v}_B &= \mathbf{R}_{BA} \cdot \vec{v}_A + \dot{\mathbf{R}}_{BA} \cdot \vec{r}_A - \mathbf{R}_{BA} \cdot \vec{v}_{O_A \to O_B}^{(A)} - \dot{\mathbf{R}}_{BA} \cdot \vec{r}_{O_A \to O_B}^{(A)}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\vec{v}_{O_A \to O_B}^{(A)}$ is the velocity of the origin of system B relative to the origin of system A, expressed in system A.

::: tip Acceleration Transformation
If acceleration transformation is required, Coriolis and centripetal acceleration terms must also be included:

$$
\vec{a}_B = \mathbf{R}_{BA} \cdot \vec{a}_A - 2\,\vec{\omega}_{BA} \times \vec{v}_B - \dot{\vec{\omega}}_{BA} \times \vec{r}_B - \vec{\omega}_{BA} \times (\vec{\omega}_{BA} \times \vec{r}_B)
$$
:::

::: tip Notation
$\vec{a}_A$ is the absolute acceleration in the inertial frame (system A), and $\vec{a}_B$ is the relative acceleration in the rotating frame (system B).
:::

## Common Transformation Matrices

::: tip Important Convention
In this section, the matrix notation $\mathbf{R}_{\text{Target} \leftarrow \text{Source}}$ indicates transformation of a vector from the source coordinate system to the target coordinate system. Matrix multiplication follows the transformation chain direction from right to left.
:::

### GCRF to J2000 (Frame Bias)

GCRF (Geocentric Celestial Reference Frame) is the geocentric realization of ICRF, with no rotation (translation only). J2000 is the coordinate system defined by the mean equator and mean equinox at the J2000.0 epoch (JD 2451545.0 TDB).

The transformation between them is a **constant frame bias matrix** $\mathbf{B}$:

$$
\mathbf{R}_{J2000 \leftarrow GCRF} = \mathbf{B}
$$

The frame bias arises from:
- The offset between the mean equinox at J2000.0 and the ICRF equinox direction
- The tilt between the mean equator plane at J2000.0 and the ICRF equator plane, with a magnitude of approximately **~17 milliarcseconds** (mas), corresponding to a position error of approximately **~0.08 m** at 1000 km orbital altitude

For applications with modest accuracy requirements (e.g., preliminary mission analysis), $\mathbf{B} \approx \mathbf{I}$ may be used as an approximation; for precision orbit determination and high-accuracy pointing, it must be included.

### J2000 to MeanOfDate (Precession)

MeanOfDate (MOD) is defined by the mean equator and mean equinox at the current epoch, incorporating only precession effects (excluding nutation). Using the IAU 2006 precession theory (which supersedes the earlier IAU 1976 model):

$$
\mathbf{R}_{MOD \leftarrow J2000} = \mathbf{P}(t)
$$

The classical 3-2-3 precession angle parameterization is:

$$
\mathbf{R}_{MOD \leftarrow J2000} = \mathbf{R}_z(-z_A) \cdot \mathbf{R}_y(\theta_A) \cdot \mathbf{R}_z(-\zeta_A)
$$

where $\zeta_A, \theta_A, z_A$ are the precession angles (Capitaine et al., 2003), computed from the polynomial model of the IAU 2006 precession theory, with time $t$ expressed in Julian centuries since J2000.0.

::: warning Note
The IAU 2006 precession model is approximately two orders of magnitude more accurate than the IAU 1976 model (~0.1 mas vs ~20 mas cumulative error).
:::

### MeanOfDate to TrueOfDate (Nutation)

TrueOfDate (TOD) is defined by the instantaneous (true) equator and instantaneous (true) equinox at the current epoch, incorporating nutation effects:

$$
\mathbf{R}_{TOD \leftarrow MOD} = \mathbf{N}(t)
$$

The classical parameterization is:

$$
\mathbf{R}_{TOD \leftarrow MOD} = \mathbf{R}_x(-\varepsilon_A - \Delta\varepsilon) \cdot \mathbf{R}_z(-\Delta\psi) \cdot \mathbf{R}_x(\varepsilon_A)
$$

where:
- $\varepsilon_A$: Mean obliquity of the ecliptic (given by the precession theory; approximately $23^\circ 26'21.''4$ in the IAU 2006 model)
- $\Delta\psi$: Nutation in longitude
- $\Delta\varepsilon$: Nutation in obliquity

$\Delta\psi$ and $\Delta\varepsilon$ are computed from the IAU 2000A/2006 nutation model (containing 1000+ terms) or the simplified IAU 2000B model (~77 terms).

### TrueOfDate to Fixed (Earth Rotation and Polar Motion)

Transforming from TOD to the Earth-fixed frame (ITRF) requires Earth rotation and polar motion corrections:

$$
\mathbf{R}_{Fixed \leftarrow TOD} = \mathbf{W}(t) \cdot \mathbf{R}_z(GAST)
$$

where:
- $GAST$: Greenwich Apparent Sidereal Time
  $GAST = GMST + \Delta\psi \cos\varepsilon_A$
  $GMST$ is Greenwich Mean Sidereal Time, computed from UT1 time
- $\mathbf{W}(t)$: Polar motion matrix
  $\mathbf{W}(t) = \mathbf{R}_y(-x_p) \cdot \mathbf{R}_x(-y_p)$
  $x_p, y_p$ are polar motion components (in arcseconds, published by IERS), defining the position of the Celestial Intermediate Pole (CIP) in ITRF

::: warning Note
If using the IAU 2006/2000A CIO-based method, steps 2-4 above are replaced by two steps (see Section 6).
:::

### Complete GCRF to Fixed Transformation (Earth)

Combining the above steps, the complete transformation chain from GCRF to the Earth-fixed frame is:

$$
\boxed{\mathbf{R}_{Fixed \leftarrow GCRF} = \mathbf{W} \cdot \mathbf{R}_z(GAST) \cdot \mathbf{N} \cdot \mathbf{P} \cdot \mathbf{B}}
$$

Or expanded as the explicit product of individual matrices:

$$
\mathbf{R}_{Fixed \leftarrow GCRF} = \mathbf{R}_y(-x_p) \cdot \mathbf{R}_x(-y_p) \cdot \mathbf{R}_z(GAST) \cdot \mathbf{R}_x(-\varepsilon_A - \Delta\varepsilon) \cdot \mathbf{R}_z(-\Delta\psi) \cdot \mathbf{R}_x(\varepsilon_A) \cdot \mathbf{R}_z(-z_A) \cdot \mathbf{R}_y(\theta_A) \cdot \mathbf{R}_z(-\zeta_A) \cdot \mathbf{B}
$$

### CIO-Based Transformation Method (Modern Alternative)

The IAU 2006/2000A resolutions introduced a **Celestial Intermediate Origin (CIO)**-based method that avoids the complexities associated with the equinox definition. The transformation chain for this method is:

$$
\mathbf{R}_{TIRS \leftarrow GCRF} = \mathbf{R}_{TIRS \leftarrow CIRS} \cdot \mathbf{R}_{CIRS \leftarrow GCRF}
$$

where:
- **CIRS** (Celestial Intermediate Reference System): Defined by the CIO and CIP (Celestial Intermediate Pole)
- **TIRS** (Terrestrial Intermediate Reference System): Defined by the TIO (Terrestrial Intermediate Origin) and CIP
- $\mathbf{R}_{CIRS \leftarrow GCRF}$ is determined by the CIP coordinates ($X, Y$) and the Celestial Intermediate Origin locator angle ($s$)
- $\mathbf{R}_{TIRS \leftarrow CIRS} = \mathbf{R}_z(ERA)$, where $ERA$ is the Earth Rotation Angle, a linear function of UT1

The CIO-based method is superior to the traditional equinox-based method in both accuracy and conceptual clarity, and is the preferred choice for modern high-precision applications.

::: tip Note
ATK internally uses the IAU 2006 CIO-based method by default, while maintaining compatibility with the traditional equinox-based method.
:::

### TEME Frame and Its Transformation

**TEME** (True Equator Mean Equinox) is the standard output frame for the SGP4/SDP4 orbit propagation models. It is defined as:
- Equator: **Instantaneous true equator** (same as TOD)
- Equinox: **Mean equinox** (same as MOD)

The relationship between TEME and TOD involves only the "equation of the equinoxes":

$$
\mathbf{R}_{TOD \leftarrow TEME} = \mathbf{R}_z(\Delta\psi \cos\varepsilon_A)
$$

## Euler Angles and Rotation Sequences

### Definition of Euler Angles

Euler angles are three independent angles describing the attitude of a rigid body, achieving arbitrary orientation through three successive rotations about the coordinate axes. Euler angles have three degrees of freedom, consistent with the dimension of the SO(3) group.

Common rotation sequences:

| Sequence | Alias | Common Application | Rotation Order |
|----------|-------|-------------------|----------------|
| **3-1-3** (Z-X-Z) | Classical Euler angles | Celestial mechanics, orbital mechanics | Precession → Nutation → Spin |
| **3-2-1** (Z-Y-X) | Tait-Bryan, Yaw-Pitch-Roll | Aviation, spacecraft attitude | Yaw → Pitch → Roll |
| **1-2-3** (X-Y-Z) | Roll-Pitch-Yaw | Some spacecraft | Roll → Pitch → Yaw |
| **3-1-2** (Z-X-Y) | — | Some attitude determination systems | — |

::: tip Axis Numbering Convention
1 = x-axis, 2 = y-axis, 3 = z-axis. Rotation $R_i(\theta)$ denotes rotation by angle $\theta$ about the $i$-th axis, with positive direction determined by the right-hand rule.
:::

### Basic Rotation Matrices

All rotation matrices in this text follow the passive rotation definition: the coordinate system rotates while the physical vector remains fixed. For active rotation (vector rotation with fixed coordinate system), use the transpose matrix $\mathbf{R}^T$.

The basic rotation matrices about the coordinate axes are:

$$
\mathbf{R}_x(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ 0 & -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{R}_y(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & 0 & -\sin\theta \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ \sin\theta & 0 & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{R}_z(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta & 0 \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

### Euler Angles to DCM

Using the 3-2-1 sequence as an example (angles $\psi$: yaw, $\theta$: pitch, $\phi$: roll):

$$
\mathbf{R}_{321}(\psi, \theta, \phi) = \mathbf{R}_x(\phi) \cdot \mathbf{R}_y(\theta) \cdot \mathbf{R}_z(\psi)
$$

Expanded form:

$$
\mathbf{R}_{321} = \begin{bmatrix}
\cos\theta \cos\psi & \cos\theta \sin\psi & -\sin\theta \\[4pt]
\sin\phi \sin\theta \cos\psi - \cos\phi \sin\psi & \sin\phi \sin\theta \sin\psi + \cos\phi \cos\psi & \sin\phi \cos\theta \\[4pt]
\cos\phi \sin\theta \cos\psi + \sin\phi \sin\psi & \cos\phi \sin\theta \sin\psi - \sin\phi \cos\psi & \cos\phi \cos\theta
\end{bmatrix}
$$

### DCM to Euler Angles

Using the 3-2-1 sequence as an example (extracting Euler angles from matrix elements):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\psi &= \operatorname{atan2}(R_{12}, R_{11}) \\[4pt]
\theta &= -\arcsin(R_{13}) \quad (\text{or } \operatorname{atan2}(-R_{13}, \sqrt{R_{11}^2 + R_{12}^2})) \\[4pt]
\phi &= \operatorname{atan2}(R_{23}, R_{33})
\end{aligned}
$$

::: warning Gimbal Lock
When $\theta = \pm 90°$, $\cos\theta = 0$, the matrix degenerates to a form that depends only on $\psi \pm \phi$ (or $\psi \mp \phi$), and $\psi$ and $\phi$ cannot be independently determined — this is an inherent singularity of Euler angles. For applications where the pitch angle approaches 90°, quaternions or direction cosine matrices are recommended to avoid singularities.
:::

## Quaternion Transformation

### Quaternion Definition

A quaternion $\mathbf{q} = [q_s,\ q_x,\ q_y,\ q_z]^\top = [q_s,\ \mathbf{q}_v]^\top$ satisfies the normalization condition:

$$
q_s^2 + q_x^2 + q_y^2 + q_z^2 = 1
$$

where $q_s = \cos(\Phi/2)$ is the scalar part, $\mathbf{q}_v = \sin(\Phi/2)\,\hat{\mathbf{e}}$ is the vector part, $\Phi$ is the rotation angle, and $\hat{\mathbf{e}}$ is the unit vector along the rotation axis.

::: tip Note
Quaternions are the most compact singularity-free representation of rotations (4 parameters vs. 9 for DCM vs. 3 for Euler angles) and are widely used in spacecraft attitude determination and control.
:::

### Quaternion to DCM

$$
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{q}) = \begin{bmatrix}
q_s^2+q_x^2-q_y^2-q_z^2 & 2(q_x q_y+q_s q_z) & 2(q_x q_z-q_s q_y) \\[4pt]
2(q_x q_y-q_s q_z) & q_s^2-q_x^2+q_y^2-q_z^2 & 2(q_y q_z+q_s q_x) \\[4pt]
2(q_x q_z+q_s q_y) & 2(q_y q_z-q_s q_x) & q_s^2-q_x^2-q_y^2+q_z^2
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Equivalent compact form:

$$
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{q}) = (q_s^2 - \|\mathbf{q}_v\|^2)\mathbf{I} + 2\mathbf{q}_v \mathbf{q}_v^\top + 2q_s[\mathbf{q}_v]^\times
$$

where $[\mathbf{q}_v]^\times$ is the skew-symmetric cross-product matrix of vector $\mathbf{q}_v$.

### DCM to Quaternion

**Standard Method** (Shepperd's algorithm, numerically robust):

Compute the four candidate values and select the largest:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q_s^2 &= \tfrac{1}{4}(1 + R_{11} + R_{22} + R_{33}) \\
q_x^2 &= \tfrac{1}{4}(1 + R_{11} - R_{22} - R_{33}) \\
q_y^2 &= \tfrac{1}{4}(1 - R_{11} + R_{22} - R_{33}) \\
q_z^2 &= \tfrac{1}{4}(1 - R_{11} - R_{22} + R_{33})
\end{aligned}
$$

Select the $q_i^2$ with the largest value as the pivot, then determine the remaining three components from known relationships. For example, if $q_s$ is largest:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q_s &= \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{1+R_{11}+R_{22}+R_{33}} \\[3pt]
q_x &= \frac{R_{23}-R_{32}}{4q_s}, \quad
q_y = \frac{R_{31}-R_{13}}{4q_s}, \quad
q_z = \frac{R_{12}-R_{21}}{4q_s}
\end{aligned}
$$

::: warning Numerical Note
When $q_s \to 0$ (corresponding to a ~180° rotation), the denominators in the above formulas approach zero, leading to loss of precision. Shepperd's algorithm avoids this issue by selecting the largest component as the denominator.
:::

### Quaternion Multiplication and Successive Rotations

Quaternion multiplication $\otimes$ corresponds to the composition of rotations (note the non-commutativity of multiplication):

$$
\mathbf{q}_{CA} = \mathbf{q}_{CB} \otimes \mathbf{q}_{BA}
$$

Specifically defined as:

$$
\mathbf{p} \otimes \mathbf{q} = \begin{bmatrix}
p_s q_s - \mathbf{p}_v \cdot \mathbf{q}_v \\
p_s \mathbf{q}_v + q_s \mathbf{p}_v + \mathbf{p}_v \times \mathbf{q}_v
\end{bmatrix}
$$

::: warning Note
Different references may use different conventions for quaternion multiplication order (e.g., the JPL convention where $\mathbf{q} \otimes \mathbf{p}$ means apply $\mathbf{p}$ first, then $\mathbf{q}$). Always verify the convention used by the specific software/library.
:::

## Coordinate Chain Transformations

In practice, it is often necessary to complete transformations through multiple intermediate coordinate systems.

### Chain Rule

For a coordinate chain $A \rightarrow B \rightarrow C \rightarrow D$:

$$
\mathbf{R}_{DA} = \mathbf{R}_{DC} \cdot \mathbf{R}_{CB} \cdot \mathbf{R}_{BA}
$$

Note the **right-to-left** order of matrix multiplication: the rightmost matrix corresponds to the first transformation step, and the leftmost matrix corresponds to the last transformation step.

### Typical Transformation Chain Examples

1. **GCRF → Trajectory-Based Frame (LVLH)**

   The LVLH frame is constructed directly from the spacecraft's position and velocity vectors in GCRF (or Fixed). The most direct method is through geometric definitions:

   **Procedure**: Given the spacecraft's position $\vec{r}$ and velocity $\vec{v}$ in GCRF ($\vec{r}$ relative to the central body), the LVLH basis vectors (expressed in GCRF) are:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \hat{z}_{LVLH}^{(GCRF)} &= -\frac{\vec{r}}{\|\vec{r}\|} \quad (\text{pointing toward the central body}) \\[4pt]
   \hat{y}_{LVLH}^{(GCRF)} &= -\frac{\vec{r} \times \vec{v}}{\|\vec{r} \times \vec{v}\|} \quad (\text{pointing along the negative orbital plane normal}) \\[4pt]
   \hat{x}_{LVLH}^{(GCRF)} &= \hat{y}_{LVLH} \times \hat{z}_{LVLH} \quad (\text{approximately in-track; exactly in-track only for circular orbits})
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The transformation matrix is then:

   $$
   \mathbf{R}_{LVLH \leftarrow GCRF} = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{x}^\top \\ \hat{y}^\top \\ \hat{z}^\top \end{bmatrix}_{3\times3}
   $$

   (Each row is a basis vector expressed in GCRF components)

2. **GCRF → VNC**

   The basis vectors of the VNC frame are constructed in GCRF as follows:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \hat{x}_{VNC}^{(GCRF)} &= \frac{\vec{v}}{\|\vec{v}\|} \quad (\text{velocity direction}) \\[4pt]
   \hat{y}_{VNC}^{(GCRF)} &= \frac{\vec{r} \times \vec{v}}{\|\vec{r} \times \vec{v}\|} \quad (\text{orbital plane normal}) \\[4pt]
   \hat{z}_{VNC}^{(GCRF)} &= \hat{x}_{VNC} \times \hat{y}_{VNC} \quad (\text{co-normal})
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. **GCRF → Ground Station Local Frame (NED)**

   Given the ground station's geodetic coordinates in the Fixed frame (latitude $\varphi$, longitude $\lambda$, altitude $h$), first construct the NED basis vectors in the Fixed frame:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \hat{n}^{(Fixed)} &= [-\sin\varphi\cos\lambda,\ -\sin\varphi\sin\lambda,\ \cos\varphi]^\top \\[3pt]
   \hat{e}^{(Fixed)} &= [-\sin\lambda,\ \cos\lambda,\ 0]^\top \\[3pt]
   \hat{d}^{(Fixed)} &= [-\cos\varphi\cos\lambda,\ -\cos\varphi\sin\lambda,\ -\sin\varphi]^\top
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   Then the NED → Fixed matrix is $[\hat{n}, \hat{e}, \hat{d}]_{3\times3}^\top$, and NED → GCRF is:

   $$
   \mathbf{R}_{NED \leftarrow GCRF} = \mathbf{R}_{NED \leftarrow Fixed} \cdot \mathbf{R}_{Fixed \leftarrow GCRF}
   $$

## Time Derivatives and Velocity Transformation

### Velocity in a Rotating Frame

When coordinate system B rotates relative to coordinate system A with angular velocity $\vec{\omega}_{BA}$, the velocity transformation includes a transport velocity term:

$$
\vec{v}_B = \mathbf{R}_{BA} \cdot \vec{v}_A + \vec{\omega}_{BA} \times \vec{r}_B
$$

or equivalently:

$$
\vec{v}_B = \mathbf{R}_{BA} \cdot \vec{v}_A + \vec{\omega}_{BA} \times (\mathbf{R}_{BA} \cdot \vec{r}_A)
$$

### Angular Velocity Vector and DCM Time Derivative

The angular velocity $\vec{\omega}_{BA}$ (components expressed in system B) is determined by the time derivative of the DCM.

**Correct Formula**: Define the skew-symmetric matrix $\boldsymbol{\Omega} = \dot{\mathbf{R}}_{BA} \mathbf{R}_{BA}^\top$, then:

$$
\boldsymbol{\Omega} = [\vec{\omega}_{BA}]^\times = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & -\omega_z & \omega_y \\
\omega_z & 0 & -\omega_x \\
-\omega_y & \omega_x & 0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Extracting the angular velocity components:

$$
\vec{\omega}_{BA}^{(B)} = \begin{bmatrix}
\dot{\mathbf{R}}_{3,:} \cdot \mathbf{R}_{2,:} \\[3pt]
\dot{\mathbf{R}}_{1,:} \cdot \mathbf{R}_{3,:} \\[3pt]
\dot{\mathbf{R}}_{2,:} \cdot \mathbf{R}_{1,:}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

where $\mathbf{R}_{i,:}$ denotes the $i$-th row vector of $\mathbf{R}$. That is:

$$
\omega_x = \sum_k \dot{R}_{3k} R_{2k},\quad
\omega_y = \sum_k \dot{R}_{1k} R_{3k},\quad
\omega_z = \sum_k \dot{R}_{2k} R_{1k}
$$

::: warning Common Pitfall
The formula $\omega = [\dot{R}_{32}-\dot{R}_{23},\ \dot{R}_{13}-\dot{R}_{31},\ \dot{R}_{21}-\dot{R}_{12}]^\top$ is only valid under the small-angle approximation when $\mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{I}$ (where $\dot{\mathbf{R}}$ is itself nearly skew-symmetric). It is generally not valid for finite rotations.
:::

### Typical Angular Velocity Reference Values

| Coordinate Transformation | Angular Velocity Magnitude | Description |
|---------------------------|---------------------------|-------------|
| Fixed ← TOD | ~7.292 × 10⁻⁵ rad/s | Earth's rotation rate $\omega_\oplus$ |
| TOD ← MOD | ≪ 10⁻¹⁰ rad/s | Nutation rate, extremely slow |
| MOD ← J2000 | ~7.7 × 10⁻¹² rad/s | Precession rate |
| LVLH ← GCRF (LEO) | ~1.1 × 10⁻³ rad/s | Orbital angular rate (altitude ~400 km) |
| LVLH ← GCRF (GEO) | ~7.3 × 10⁻⁵ rad/s | Synchronous with Earth's rotation |

## Coordinate Transformation in ATK

### Using the Vector Geometry Tool (VGT)

In ATK, arbitrary coordinate systems can be defined and used through the VGT, with the system automatically handling coordinate transformations.

```
# Pseudo-code example: Defining coordinate transformations in VGT
frame_a = CoordinateSystem("ICRF")
frame_b = CoordinateSystem("Earth.Fixed")
frame_c = CoordinateSystem("Satellite1.LVLH")

# Obtain transformation matrices
R_ba = get_transform(frame_a, frame_b)
R_ca = get_transform(frame_a, frame_c)
```

### Using in Scripts

ATK's scripting interface provides coordinate transformation capabilities:

```python
# Python pseudo-code example
from atk import CoordinateSystem, transform

# Define coordinate systems
icrf = CoordinateSystem("ICRF")
j2000 = CoordinateSystem("J2000")
fixed = CoordinateSystem("Earth.Fixed")

# Vector transformation
v_icrf = [1000, 2000, 3000]  # km
v_fixed = transform(v_icrf, from=icrf, to=fixed, epoch="2025-01-01T00:00:00Z")
```

### Coordinate System References in Files

In ATK data files, coordinate systems are referenced via keywords:

- `.a` files (attitude files): Use the `CoordinateAxes` keyword
- `.e` files (ephemeris files): Use the `CoordinateSystem` keyword

Example:
```
CoordinateAxes ICRF
CoordinateAxes Satellite1.LVLH
CoordinateSystem Earth.Fixed
```

## Accuracy Considerations in Coordinate Transformation

### Primary Error Sources

1. **Model errors**: Accuracy limitations of precession, nutation, and polar motion models
2. **Numerical errors**: Accumulated round-off errors from matrix operations
3. **Time system errors**: Differences between time systems (UT1, UTC, TDB, TT)
4. **Frame bias**: The small rotation between ICRF and J2000

### Accuracy Recommendations

| Application Type | Recommended Accuracy | Factors to Consider |
|------------------|---------------------|---------------------|
| Preliminary mission analysis | ~1 km | Use simplified models |
| Standard orbit design | ~100 m | Standard precession/nutation models |
| Precision orbit determination | ~1 m | Full models + polar motion correction |
| High-accuracy pointing | ~1 cm | Include frame bias and relativistic effects |

### Common Issues

**Issue 1: Significant position error after transformation**
- Check: Was the time system difference handled correctly?
- Check: Was polar motion correction applied?
- Check: Is the epoch setting correct?

**Issue 2: Velocity magnitude changes after transformation**
- Normal phenomenon: Velocity in a rotating frame includes the transport velocity term
- Check: Was the $\vec{\omega} \times \vec{r}$ term computed correctly?

**Issue 3: Direction drift after long-term propagation**
- Cause: Loss of orthogonality of the direction cosine matrix in numerical integration
- Solution: Periodic re-orthogonalization (Gram-Schmidt or SVD)

## Reference Frame Implementation Matrix Quick Reference

The table below gives the transformation matrices between intermediate coordinate systems from GCRF to Earth-fixed frame.
Matrix direction is defined as $\mathbf{R}_{\text{Target}\leftarrow\text{Source}}$, i.e., element $M_{\text{row},\text{column}}$ in the table represents the rotation matrix from the **row frame** to the **column frame**.
All origin translations are ignored; only pure rotations are included.

| From \ To | GCRF | J2000 | MOD | TOD | Fixed |
|-----------|------|-------|-----|-----|-------|
| **GCRF**  | I | $\mathbf{B}$ | $\mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{B}$ | $\mathbf{N}\,\mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{B}$ | $\mathbf{W}\,\mathbf{R}_z(\text{GAST})\,\mathbf{N}\,\mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{B}$ |
| **J2000** | $\mathbf{B}^\top$ | I | $\mathbf{P}$ | $\mathbf{N}\,\mathbf{P}$ | $\mathbf{W}\,\mathbf{R}_z(\text{GAST})\,\mathbf{N}\,\mathbf{P}$ |
| **MOD**   | $\mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{P}^\top$ | $\mathbf{P}^\top$ | I | $\mathbf{N}$ | $\mathbf{W}\,\mathbf{R}_z(\text{GAST})\,\mathbf{N}$ |
| **TOD**   | $\mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{P}^\top\mathbf{N}^\top$ | $\mathbf{P}^\top\mathbf{N}^\top$ | $\mathbf{N}^\top$ | I | $\mathbf{W}\,\mathbf{R}_z(\text{GAST})$ |
| **Fixed** | Transpose of full chain | $\mathbf{P}^\top\mathbf{N}^\top\mathbf{R}_z^\top\mathbf{W}^\top$ | $\mathbf{N}^\top\mathbf{R}_z^\top\mathbf{W}^\top$ | $\mathbf{R}_z^\top\mathbf{W}^\top$ | I |

where:
- $\mathbf{B}$: Frame bias matrix, $\mathbf{R}_{\text{J2000}\leftarrow\text{GCRF}}$ (constant, ~17 mas)
- $\mathbf{P}$: Precession matrix, $\mathbf{R}_{\text{MOD}\leftarrow\text{J2000}}$ (IAU 2006 model)
- $\mathbf{N}$: Nutation matrix, $\mathbf{R}_{\text{TOD}\leftarrow\text{MOD}}$ (IAU 2000A/2006 model)
- $\mathbf{R}_z(\text{GAST})$: Rotation about the z-axis by Greenwich Apparent Sidereal Time (Earth rotation)
- $\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}_y(-x_p)\,\mathbf{R}_x(-y_p)$: Polar motion matrix (determined by $x_p, y_p$ published by IERS)
- $\top$ denotes matrix transpose (inverse of the rotation matrix)

::: tip Note
- "GCRF" in the table is equivalent to the geocentric realization of ICRF; the rotational part is identical.
- If polar motion accuracy is not required, $\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{I}$ may be used; in this case, Fixed is approximately TOD rotated about z by GAST.
- The equation of the equinoxes correction $R_E$ related to TEME is not included in this table; this correction is only used between TEME ↔ TOD.
- In practice, it is recommended to directly call ATK's built-in coordinate transformation interface to ensure all auxiliary corrections (e.g., polar motion, time systems) are handled correctly.
:::
